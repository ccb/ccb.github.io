Introduction

Instruction-tuned vision-language models (VLMs) have shown strong performance across a range of multimodal tasks (Radford et al., 2021; OpenAI, 2023; Liu et al., 2023). However, these tasks typically focus on general image understanding over natural images rather than the specialized reasoning required for text-rich images such as charts, documents, diagrams, signs, labels, and screenshots. Understanding and reasoning over text-rich images is crucial for many applications, including analyzing scientific literature and figures (Asai et al., 2024), improving accessibility for users with visual impairments (Gurari et al., 2018), and enabling agentic workflows in real-world environments (Xie et al., 2024). Effectively interpreting these structured visual formats requires both textual comprehension and spatial reasoning, which current models struggle with due to the limited availability of high-quality, realistic, and diverse vision-language datasets (Methani et al., 2020).

To address these challenges, and inspired by the fact that text-rich images are typically rendered from code, we develop CoSyn (Code-Guided Synthetic data generation), a flexible framework for generating diverse synthetic text-rich multimodal data for vision-language instruction tuning. As illustrated in Figure 2, CoSyn can generate multimodal data for various target domains from a short natural language query, such as book covers. CoSyn leverages text-only LLMs, which excel at code generation, to produce both data and code that render diverse text-rich images using 11 supported rendering tools (e.g., Python, HTML, LaTeX). Grounded in the underlying code representation of the images, textual instructions are also generated by the text-only LLM to create vision-language instruction-tuning datasets.

Using this framework, we construct CoSyn-400K (Figure 3), a large-scale and diverse synthetic vision-language instruction-tuning dataset tailored for text-rich image understanding. We comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of training on CoSyn-generated data across seven text-rich VQA benchmarks. Our model achieves state-of-the-art performance among competitive open-source models and surpasses proprietary models such as GPT-4V and Gemini 1.5. Notably, training on CoSyn synthetic data enables sample-efficient learning, achieving stronger performance with less data. In addition, CoSyn can synthesize chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning data (Wei et al., 2022), improving performance on tasks requiring multi-hop reasoning. A fine-grained analysis of question types in ChartQA (Masry et al., 2022) reveals that training on CoSyn-400K results in stronger generalization to human-written questions. In contrast, models trained solely on existing academic datasets often overfit to biased training data, overperforming on templated or machine-generated questions but struggling with more realistic, human-asked queries.

We then identify a key limitation of open-source VLMs: they struggle to generalize to out-of-domain tasks they were not trained on. As shown in Figure 1, we introduce NutritionQA, a novel benchmark for understanding photos of nutrition labels, with practical applications like aiding users with visual impairments. Open-source VLMs perform poorly on this novel task, even after training on millions of images. However, by training on CoSyn-400K, our model adapts strongly to this novel domain in a zero-shot setting with significantly less training data. Remarkably, by generating just 7K in-domain synthetic nutrition-label examples with CoSyn for fine-tuning, our model surpasses most open VLMs trained on millions of images. This highlights CoSyn's data efficiency and ability to help VLMs adapt to new domains through targeted synthetic data generation.

Finally, beyond the standard VQA task, we use CoSyn to generate synthetic pointing training data, which is particularly useful in agentic tasks. The pointing data enables VLMs to retrieve coordinates for specific elements in a screenshot given a query like "Point to the Checkout button" (Deitke et al., 2024). Our model trained on synthetic pointing data achieves state-of-the-art performance on the ScreenSpot click-prediction benchmark (Baechler et al., 2024). Overall, our work demonstrates that synthetic data is a promising solution for advancing vision-language models in understanding text-rich images and unlocking their potential as multimodal digital assistants for real-world applications.

Related Work

Vision-Language Models

Tsimpoukelli et al. (2021) first demonstrated that pre-trained, frozen language models can be extended to process visual inputs. Prior works fuse vision and language modalities using different strategies, such as cross-attention mechanisms (Alayrac et al., 2022) and Q-Former (Li et al., 2023). More recent architectures have converged on using MLP layers to project visual features into the language space (Liu et al., 2023). However, these architectures are often imbalanced, with the language backbone substantially larger than the visual encoder. As a result, without high-quality image-text data, models may over-rely on language priors, leading to hallucinations in their responses (Bai et al., 2024). Our work addresses this issue by generating high-quality multimodal data for text-rich images.

Text-Rich Image Understanding

Chart understanding and text-rich image understanding continue to challenge state-of-the-art models, as naturally occurring vision-language data that can support training for such images is still scarce (Kahou et al., 2017; Kafle et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2023; Mukhopadhyay et al., 2024). In addition to charts and plots, a number of datasets address other kinds of text-rich images such as documents, infographics, diagrams, figures, and screenshots (Siegel et al., 2016; Mathew et al., 2021, 2022; Baechler et al., 2024; Roberts et al., 2024). Many of these benchmarks are limited in size and scope, diversity of visualization types, and question types, making them suitable for evaluation but not for training data that could lead to generalized performance.

Synthetic Data for VLMs

Generating synthetic images with annotations grounded in known source representations has been widely used in domains with limited vision-language data (Richter et al., 2016; Johnson et al., 2017; Cascante-Bonilla et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024). This approach has been applied to chart and plot VQA, typically using a limited, small set of chart types and by instantiating handcrafted question templates (Kahou et al., 2017; Kafle et al., 2018; Methani et al., 2020; Chaudhry et al., 2020). Following this, Li and Tajbakhsh (2023) and Carbune et al. (2024) explored using text-only LLMs to generate annotations from tables or text descriptions associated with charts to train VLMs. Other recent approaches, similar to our procedure, explore generating data and code to render synthetic charts (Han et al., 2023; He et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024). Molmo (Deitke et al., 2024) releases a synthetic text-rich image dataset, PixMo-docs, but smaller in scale and diversity than ours. These works generate synthetic data that is still highly limited in terms of the diversity of topics, figure types, and rendering pipelines, which is important for generalizing to out-of-distribution tasks. We expand the scope beyond charts to encompass a wider range of diverse text-rich images.

Problem Formulation

Given a text query $q$ about an image type (e.g., flow charts), our goal is to create a synthetic multimodal dataset $\mathcal{D}_q = \{(I, T)\}$, where $I$ is the image and $T$ is the textual instruction-tuning data (e.g., question-answer pairs). $\mathcal{D}_q$ is used to train a VLM to improve its ability to understand images related to $q$. The core idea of our approach is using code $C$ as the intermediate representation to bridge the image and text. The overall generation process can be decomposed as follows:

$$ P(I, T \mid q) = P_{\text{LM}}(C \mid q) \cdot P(I \mid C) \cdot P_{\text{LM}}(T \mid C) $$

where $P_{\text{LM}}(C \mid q)$ represents prompting a language model to generate code $C$, which is executed to render the image $P(I \mid C)$. $P_{\text{LM}}(T \mid C)$ uses code $C$ (without the image) as context for an LLM to generate the textual instruction-tuning data.

The CoSyn System

Figure 2 illustrates the workflow of CoSyn. The system takes a language input, such as "generate a dataset of book covers," and outputs a multimodal dataset. Based on the input query, CoSyn selects one of 20 generation pipelines built on 11 rendering tools. The process starts with topic generation, conditioned on a sampled persona that guides the style and content. Next, the system generates data content and converts it into code, which is then executed to render synthetic images. Finally, using the code as context, we prompt the LLM to generate corresponding textual instructions.

Rendering Tools

We integrate various rendering tools to generate diverse types of images, forming the foundation of CoSyn's ability for text-rich image generation. For example, Matplotlib, Plotly, and Vega-Lite are used to create different types of charts. LaTeX and HTML are used for documents and tables, while Mermaid and Graphviz generate diagrams. We use SVG and Asymptote to create vector graphics and math-related content. For specialized tasks, we rely on Lilypond to generate music sheets and RDKit for chemical structures. We implement customized functions for each tool to execute LLM-generated code and obtain the corresponding rendered images. These tools collectively enable CoSyn to produce a wide range of high-quality, text-rich synthetic images.

Pipelines

We design 20 pipelines based on 11 rendering tools.1 Each pipeline follows the same procedure: (1) Topic generation to define the theme of the synthetic example, (2) Data generation to populate the detailed contents, (3) Code generation to create executable code that renders the image, and (4) Instruction generation conditioned on the code to produce instructions, including questions, answers, and explanations for chain-of-thought reasoning. Each stage is controlled by a prompt customized for the image category and rendering tool.

Using Personas to Enhance Diversity

LLMs often struggle to generate diverse synthetic data using sampling parameters alone (Yu et al., 2023), with biases leading to repetitive outputs across different runs. Recent work (Ge et al., 2024) shows that incorporating personas in prompts can improve diversity by enabling models to generate from varied perspectives. CoSyn adopts personas to enhance diversity during the topic-generation stage. Each persona is a short sentence describing a personality or identity. For example, as shown in the middle of Figure 2, we sample a persona "a sci-fi novelist who likes alien worlds," which results in a topic of "a novel about Extraterrestrial Flora & Fauna" for generating the book-cover image. We use the 200K personas released by Ge et al. (2024).

Implementation Details

CoSyn is built on the DataDreamer library (Patel et al., 2024), which supports robust multi-stage synthetic data generation pipelines that are easy to maintain, reproduce, and extend. DataDreamer documents the prompts and parameters used at each generation stage and implements several efficient techniques, such as parallel generation and response caching, to optimize performance. For the data and code generation stages, we use Claude-3.5-Sonnet, which performs well on coding tasks (Anthropic, 2024). For instruction-tuning data generation, we select GPT-4o-mini for its cost efficiency.

CoSyn-400K

As shown in Figure 3, we use CoSyn to generate a large-scale synthetic dataset of 400K images across nine categories: charts, documents, math problems, tables, diagrams, vector graphics, music sheets, electrical circuits, and chemical structures. Since CoSyn is controlled via language inputs, it can easily generate diverse, fine-grained image types by varying the input queries. For instance, we use over 100 queries to generate document data covering receipts, resumes, meal plans, etc. This ensures that our dataset covers a broad range of domains.

Model Architecture

We follow the same image preprocessing and architecture as Molmo (Deitke et al., 2024), which uses an MLP layer to connect the vision encoder and a pretrained LLM. We choose OpenAI's CLIP (ViT-L/14 336px) (Radford et al., 2021) as the vision backbone and Mistral-7B (Jiang et al., 2023) as the language model.

Training Process

We adopt Molmo's two-stage training strategy: (1) pre-training on dense captions from PixMo-Cap, and (2) supervised fine-tuning on three categories of datasets:

  • Evaluation datasets. We evaluate on seven text-rich benchmarks: ChartQA (Masry et al., 2022), DocVQA (Mathew et al., 2021), InfographicVQA (Mathew et al., 2022), TableVQA-Bench (Kim et al., 2024), AI2 Diagrams (Kembhavi et al., 2016), TextVQA (Singh et al., 2019), and ScreenQA (Baechler et al., 2024), using their official metrics. In total, we have 138K training images from the evaluation datasets.2
  • Auxiliary datasets. Additional academic datasets for fine-tuning: VQAv2, GQA, OK-VQA, OCR-VQA, A-OKVQA, ScienceQA, TabMWP, ST-VQA, TallyQA, DVQA, FigureQA, and PlotQA (around 1M training images).
  • Synthetic datasets. Our CoSyn-400K: 400K text-rich images across 9 categories.

Our best-performing model uses all three categories. We also trained a zero-shot model using only auxiliary and synthetic data without any examples from the evaluation datasets, which still exhibits competitive benchmark performance (last row of Table 1).

Baselines and Implementation

We compare recent open-source VLMs of similar scale (~7B): PaliGemma-3B (Beyer et al., 2024), BLIP-3-4B (Xue et al., 2024), Cambrian-7B (Tong et al., 2024), LLaVA-1.5-7B (Liu et al., 2023), LLaVA-Next-8B (Liu et al., 2024), LLaVA-OneVision-7B (Li et al., 2024), Pixtral-12B (Agrawal et al., 2024), and Llama 3.2 V (Meta, 2024). We also include proprietary models: GPT-4V, Gemini-1.5-Flash (Gemini Team, 2024), and Claude-3 Opus (Anthropic, 2024). We train our model on TPU v3-128 with a batch size of 32; the best model is trained for 60K steps, taking about 30 hours, retaining the checkpoints with the highest validation performance.

Results

Main Results

Table 1 compares our model's performance with both open and closed models across seven text-rich benchmarks. On average, our 7B model achieves the highest performance, surpassing the second-best model (Llama 3.2 11B) by 3.9%. Notably, our model ranks first in four of the seven datasets and second in the remaining three. More surprisingly, our zero-shot model (last row of Table 1) outperforms most open and closed models without exposure to any training instances from the evaluation datasets. In contrast, these competing models often rely on benchmark training data and are thus not true zero-shot models. This demonstrates that the capabilities learned from our synthetic data transfer effectively to downstream tasks.

Analysis

Synthetic data boosts performance. Figure 4 presents an ablation on the choice of supervised fine-tuning data. In the zero-shot setting, when the model is trained on auxiliary datasets alone (over 1M training images not directly from the evaluation tasks), it fails to generalize effectively, with a substantial gap of 14.1% below GPT-4V. However, using only 400K synthetic samples achieves performance comparable to GPT-4V, and our best zero-shot model surpasses GPT-4V when jointly training synthetic and auxiliary data. Under the supervised setting, training with in-domain data alone yields strong performance; adding 1M auxiliary samples provides a modest 1.4% improvement, while incorporating synthetic data results in a more significant 3.6% boost.

Zero-shot generalization on a novel task. Vision-language models typically rely on in-domain data to perform well. When encountering a novel task, such as answering questions about nutrition labels (Figure 1), models that have not seen similar examples during training may struggle. CoSyn, however, enables controllable data generation: given the task name as input, it can generate task-specific data to fine-tune the model. To validate this, we annotated a small evaluation set, NutritionQA, with 100 examples of questions about photos of nutrition labels; some questions require multi-hop reasoning. Despite being trained on millions of images, open-source VLMs are not data-efficient and perform poorly on this novel task compared to GPT-4V. Without synthetic data, our model (Eval + Aux) achieves results similar to open models, but when trained on 400K synthetic samples it matches GPT-4V. More impressively, using CoSyn to generate just 7K synthetic nutrition-label samples and fine-tuning on that data alone outperforms most open-source VLMs on NutritionQA (Figure 5). Code-guided synthetic data is thus an effective and efficient method for adapting VLMs to new domains.

Synthetic data for chain-of-thought reasoning. Existing text-rich datasets such as ChartQA are typically annotated with short answers. However, questions like "Compute the mean of the data in the plot" require step-by-step reasoning. Models trained only with short-answer supervision may fail to learn proper plot comprehension and instead overfit to annotation biases. CoSyn-400K includes explanation text alongside the short answer: each instruction-tuning example is a (question, explanation, short answer) triplet, enabling models to learn CoT reasoning. During fine-tuning we design two prompt templates:

CoT Prompt: <Question> Provide reasoning steps and then give the short answer. <Explanation> Answer: <Answer>

Short Answer Prompt: <Question> Answer with as few words as possible. <Answer>

These prompts allow VLMs to switch between answering styles and perform CoT reasoning when necessary. Figure 6 shows that incorporating CoT reasoning improves performance on ChartQA, TableVQA, and NutritionQA, as these datasets contain examples requiring multi-hop reasoning. However, adding CoT reasoning reduces performance on DocVQA and InfoVQA: answer biases in these benchmarks favor short responses, penalizing more verbose ones. For instance, in DocVQA the ground truth for an example is "T-Th" while the model responds "Tuesday to Thursday"; although correct, the strict string-matching metric assigns a zero score. This highlights key limitations of current multimodal benchmarks, including answering biases and rigid evaluation metrics.

Synthetic data for mitigating biases. Our experiments reveal answering biases in multimodal benchmarks that VLMs trained solely on these datasets often inherit. To validate this, we analyze ChartQA and observe a distribution shift in question types (Figure 7): some ChartQA questions are human-annotated, while others are generated by the language model T5 (Raffel et al., 2020), heavily influenced by prompt phrasing and limited to a fixed set of templates. During training, most questions (73.9%) are machine-generated, while the test set is evenly split. Models trained exclusively on ChartQA tend to overfit to T5-generated questions. As Table 2 shows, PaliGemma (Beyer et al., 2024) and ChartPali (Carbune et al., 2024) achieve high accuracy on machine-generated questions but drop over 30% on human-annotated ones. Similarly, without synthetic data our model shows a 21.8% gap between the two question types; incorporating synthetic data reduces this gap to 14.2%. This suggests that synthetic data can mitigate benchmark overfitting and enhance VLMs' usability on real-world human-asked questions.

Our synthetic data is more diverse. To quantify the diversity of images and text in our synthetic dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(I, T)\}$, we propose two metrics:

$$ \text{Diversity}(\mathcal{D})_{\text{Image}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|^2 - |\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{I_i \in \mathcal{D}} \sum_{I_j \in \mathcal{D}}^{i \neq j} \left(1 - \text{sim}(I_i, I_j)\right) $$ $$ \text{Diversity}(\mathcal{D})_{\text{Text}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|^2 - |\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{T_i \in \mathcal{D}} \sum_{T_j \in \mathcal{D}}^{i \neq j} \left(1 - \text{sim}(T_i, T_j)\right) $$

where $\text{sim}(\cdot)$ is cosine similarity. Both metrics compute the average pairwise cosine distance between the features of every instance in the dataset. For image diversity we extract features using CLIP; for text diversity we use Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) on question-answer pairs. Table 3 shows that our synthetic charts are significantly more diverse than existing datasets such as FigureQA and ChartQA, in both image and text diversity.

Scaling the size of synthetic data. In addition to diversity, the scale of synthetic data also impacts performance. As Figure 8 shows, increasing the number of synthetic chart images leads to improved ChartQA performance, demonstrating that scaling up synthetic data can further enhance VLMs on downstream tasks. Due to resource constraints, our final dataset consists of 400K images, which cost about \$8,000. Future work could explore scaling up the dataset size to push the boundaries of synthetic data's potential.

Synthetic Pointing Data

Pointing enables VLMs to answer questions by providing specific points on images, grounding their responses in visual content and letting them interact with environments — crucial for developing digital agents. We find that our code-guided system can synthesize pointing data.

Method. Since we have access to the source code for all generated images, we can prompt an LLM to modify the code to draw points explicitly. As illustrated in Figure 9, we feed the image's source code as context to the LLM, which generates a pointing question and edits the code to draw points with a predefined color. By extracting the pixel values of these points, we obtain their exact $(x, y)$ coordinates.3 We then use this pointing data to train VLMs to answer questions by providing point coordinates. In total, we generate pointing data for 65K synthetic images.

Setup. We evaluate pointing ability on ScreenSpot (Cheng et al., 2024), where the task requires models to provide the correct click location given an instruction, over screenshots from mobile phones, desktops, and web pages. We compare against a model trained on PixMo-point (Deitke et al., 2024), which consists of 155K human-annotated images; our best model uses both PixMo-point and synthetic pointing data. We also compare against CogAgent (Hong et al., 2024), SeeClick (Cheng et al., 2024), and UGround (Gou et al., 2024), the last of which is trained on 1.3M screenshots.

Results. Table 4 compares click accuracy against previous methods. Using 65K synthetic pointing samples, our model achieves performance comparable to that trained on 155K human-annotated samples. When combining synthetic and human data, our model achieves state-of-the-art performance on ScreenSpot, surpassing UGround (trained on 1.3M screenshots). These results demonstrate that synthetic pointing data is a data-efficient approach for improving VLM performance on agentic click-prediction tasks.

Conclusion

We introduced CoSyn, a framework for generating synthetic data that significantly enhances VLM performance on text-rich image understanding. Our comprehensive analysis highlights the advantages of synthetic data for domain generalization, data efficiency, and bias mitigation. This work demonstrates that the coding capabilities of text-only LLMs can effectively assist multimodal learning and unleash the potential of vision-language models for real-world applications.

Limitations

The effectiveness of synthetic data depends heavily on the quality and diversity of the prompts and rendering pipelines used for generation. For highly specialized or underrepresented domains, generating sufficiently diverse data remains challenging and may require careful prompt engineering or additional customization of rendering tools. Targeted synthetic data generation may be essential for certain tasks, and ensuring relevance and coverage still requires domain-specific expertise. Synthetic data may not fully capture the complexity of real-world data in some scenarios; improving its diversity and realism to support models in highly variable or evolving domains is a reasonable avenue for future research. Finally, our current synthetic data is limited to English and may require further extension for multilingual support.
